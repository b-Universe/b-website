Estate Tax

An estate tax is a tax imposed on the total value of a person's estate at the time of their death. It is sometimes referred to as a death tax. Although states may impose their own estate taxes in the United States, this calculator only estimates federal estate taxes (and selected states). For the context of this calculator, the definition of estate should not be confused with a common alternative definition, which is an interest in real property.

Depending on the taxable value of an estate, relatively low-valued estates won't require the filing of estate tax returns because they are below the tax exemptions threshold. For estates above the threshold, only the amounts that exceed the threshold for that year are taxable. Due to the unlimited marital deduction, the transfer of assets to a surviving U.S. citizen spouse is not taxable, and only assets transferred to other heirs are taxable.

In the United States, most people who have funds above the exemption amount ultimately won't end up paying much estate tax. The effective rate is low for several reasons. Firstly, the estate tax only applies to the portion of the estate above the exemption. Secondly, there are various legal strategies that allow people to shield their wealth from the federal government. For instance, parents sell parts of their assets to children at discounted rates and take the tax hit themselves. Trusts, explained in detail below, are also a common and viable method to reduce taxable estates.

Inheritance Tax

Upon death, a deceased person's estate is usually passed onto their heirs. An heir receives an inheritance if all or part of an estate from a recently deceased person is passed onto them. An inheritance tax is usually paid by the person inheriting the estate. The major difference between estate tax and inheritance tax is who pays the tax. The estate tax is paid based on the deceased person's estate before the money is distributed, while the inheritance tax is paid by the person inheriting the money.

While the federal government does not enforce an inheritance tax, some states enforce their own. The level of taxation applied is mainly dependent on the relationship between the deceased and the heir, and the value of the property received. In all states, inheritance from a spouse or domestic partner is exempt, while most inheriting children pay little or no inheritance tax. More distant inheritors tend to pay higher inheritance taxes.

Determining Taxable Value of an Estate

An estate is the estimated net worth of a person, which consists of their assets less any liabilities. Assets can be anything of value, such as cash, securities, real estate, insurance, trusts, annuities, and business interests. The value of these items is assessed based on fair market value, which is a reasonable amount at which the items can be purchased by interested buyers. The total fair market value of a person's assets is called a gross estate.

After the value of the assets is determined, certain liabilities or reductions may be deducted. Common liabilities include mortgages, unpaid debts, estate administration expenses, and assets passed to surviving spouses or qualified charities. After accounting for liabilities, the value of lifetime taxable gifts (any gifts made in 1977 or later) is added to this net amount, then reduced by the unified tax credit, resulting in the taxable value of the estate.

Reducing Estate Tax

There are several methods used to reduce estate taxes.

  • Use accumulated wealth. This is the quickest way to reduce the value of an estate. It is important to calculate expected life longevity and future living expenses before spending down assets.
  • Donate to charity. Any assets gifted to a qualified 501(c)3 organization avoid federal estate taxation. There is no limit on the amount that can be donated to charity.
  • Transfer to a spouse. Dying without a legal spouse can subject assets to estate tax, but if a U.S. citizen spouse inherits the assets, no estate tax is imposed due to the unlimited marital deduction.
  • Move to a new state. Including the District of Columbia, several states currently have a state estate or inheritance tax. Relocating can eliminate state-level death taxes.
  • Use an alternate valuation date. Normally, the fair market value of property in an estate is estimated on the date of death. The executor may choose an alternate valuation date six months after the date of death if it decreases both the gross amount of the estate and the estate tax liability.

Annual Gift Tax Exclusion

The Gift Tax Exclusion allows any individual to gift a set amount each year ($19,000 for 2026) to as many individuals as they desire without incurring a gift tax. Once more than this set amount is gifted to a single individual in a year, a gift tax return is mandated. A gift can be anything of value, such as cash, investments, real estate, or jewelry. Certain types of gifts are exempt from the gift tax entirely:

  • Charitable gifts.
  • Gifts to spouses.
  • Gifts to political organizations.
  • Educational expense gifts concerning tuition paid directly to the institution.
  • Medical expense gifts paid directly to the medical facility.

Unified Credit

The unified credit combines both the federal gift tax and estate tax into one system. It prevents taxpayers from giving away their entire estate during their lifetimes to avoid estate taxes.

If gifts exceed the annual gift tax exclusion, the excess amount counts against the lifetime gift tax exclusion. This reduces the unified credit available upon death. Any portion of the unified credit that is unused can be passed to a surviving spouse through portability.

Example: A person gives away $2,000,000 in taxable gifts during their lifetime and dies in 2026 when the individual federal estate tax exemption is $15,000,000. Their remaining federal estate tax exemption drops to $13,000,000.

Estate Planning & Trusts

A typical first step to estate planning is to take inventory of all assets. The next step is gathering documents, which usually includes a will dictating asset distribution. While a will provides instructions, it doesn't avoid probate. All assets must go through the state's probate process before distribution, which incurs legal, executor, and court fees.

If there are sufficient assets, the use of trusts is often recommended. A trust is a fiduciary arrangement that allows a third party, or trustee, to distribute assets according to agreed-upon conditions. It can protect heirs from creditors and offer tax protection.

There are two primary types of trusts: testamentary trusts and living trusts. Testamentary trusts are created by a will and take effect when the grantor dies. Living trusts are created and take effect during the grantor's lifetime. Living trusts allow the transfer of assets to beneficiaries after death without going through the public probate process.
