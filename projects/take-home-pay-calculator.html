It provides a detailed breakdown of your net income after factoring in 2026 federal tax brackets, standard versus itemized deductions, age bonuses, and self-employment (SECA) tax scenarios. It is fully updated to reflect the 2026 federal tax laws, including the new provisions from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA).

Federal Income Tax Brackets

FICA Taxes

FICA taxes fund Social Security and Medicare. They are automatically withheld from your paycheck.

  • Social Security Tax: You pay a 6.2% tax on your earned income, but this is strictly capped. For 2026, the wage base limit is $184,500. You won't pay Social Security tax on any income you earn above this limit.
  • Medicare Tax: You pay a 1.45% tax on all your earned income. There is no wage base limit for standard Medicare tax.
  • Additional Medicare Tax: If you earn over $200,000 as a single filer or $250,000 as a joint filer, you'll pay an extra 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on the income that exceeds those thresholds.

Pretax Deductions vs. Post-Tax Deductions

It is important to understand the difference between deductions when estimating your take-home pay.

  • FICA-Exempt Pretax Deductions: Things like health insurance premiums and HSA contributions are entirely tax-free. They bypass both federal income tax and FICA taxes.
  • FICA-Subject Pretax Deductions: Contributions to a traditional 401(k) lower your taxable income for federal taxes, but they are still subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes.
  • Deductions Not Withheld: These include things like student loan interest and traditional IRA contributions. They lower your final tax liability but aren't typically removed from your standard paycheck automatically.

Standard vs. Itemized Deductions

You can lower your taxable income by choosing either the standard deduction or itemized deductions. You'll want to pick whichever gives you the biggest tax break.

  • Standard Deduction: A flat dollar amount. For 2026, it is $16,100 for Single filers and $32,200 for Married Filing Jointly.
  • Age 65 or Older: If you are 65 or older, you get an extra $6,000 added to your standard deduction.
  • Charitable Gifts: Under new rules, if you take the standard deduction, you can still deduct up to $1,000 (Single) or $2,000 (Joint) for cash gifts made to tax-exempt charities.
  • Itemized Deductions: If your total eligible expenses (like mortgage interest, property taxes, and large medical bills) are higher than the standard deduction, you'll itemize them instead.

OBBBA Qualified Deductions

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) introduced several new deductions you can claim to reduce your adjusted gross income (AGI):

  • Tips Income: You can deduct up to $25,000 in qualified tips.
  • Overtime Compensation: You can deduct up to $12,500 (Single) or $25,000 (Joint) in qualified overtime pay.
  • Vehicle Loan Interest: You can deduct up to $10,000 in interest paid on a loan for a qualified personal passenger vehicle.

Self-Employment Taxes

If you are an independent contractor or freelancer, you don't have an employer to pay the other half of your FICA taxes. Instead, you pay the Self-Employment Contributions Act (SECA) tax.

  • The Rate: The SECA tax rate is 15.3%, consisting of 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare.
  • The Calculation: This 15.3% tax is calculated on 92.35% of your net business income.
  • The Deduction: To help offset the burden, you get to claim 50% of what you paid in self-employment tax as an above-the-line deduction, which lowers your Adjusted Gross Income for your federal income taxes.
